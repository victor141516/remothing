# HDR: stream an HDR display as HDR, or convert it to SDR well

- Status: under discussion
- Created: 2026-11-11

## Goal

When the streamed display is in HDR mode, what the client gets today is an
automatic conversion to SDR. Instead:

- a client that supports HDR should get the picture in HDR;
- a client that does not should get a better conversion to SDR than
  today's.

The user will take the decisions later; nothing is to be implemented from
this file yet. It records what the work consists of and what a first test
in the browser found (2026-11-11).

## Open questions

1. **What is wrong with today's SDR picture**, in the user's eyes: washed
   out, too dark, highlights clipped, colours off? That decides what
   "better" means and how to judge a new conversion.
2. **Which half first**: the better SDR conversion (server only, every
   client gains) or HDR to the clients that can show it?
3. **Video precision.** In the browser tested, a decoded picture reaches
   the page with 8 bits per channel whatever the stream has (see
   "Precision"). Choose:
   - a) HDR video in 8 bits with a curve of our own: most codes for the SDR
     range, the rest for highlights. Works with every codec, H.264
     included. Smooth bright gradients may show bands.
   - b) full 10 bits through a `<video>` element, which the browser shows
     in HDR by itself, but with its own brightness mapping (see "Decoded
     video"). Only fits the motion mode of task 002, where the whole
     picture is video.
   - c) both: a) in tile mode, b) in motion mode.
4. **HDR for the lossless layer too?** It is cheap: 10-bit tiles take the
   same 4 bytes per pixel as today's and reach the screen exactly (see
   "Canvas"). Without it, an area that stops moving would fall back from
   HDR video to an SDR tile.
5. **The worker.** The client draws in a worker; a canvas drawn from a
   worker only shows HDR when the page also holds a small WebGPU canvas in
   HDR mode (measured, see "Canvas"). Rely on that, or draw on the page's
   own thread while in HDR?
6. **Which browsers and devices must show HDR.** Only Edge 156 on this
   Windows PC was tested. The MacBook and the phone were not: the test page
   has to be opened on them (it needs HTTPS or localhost). How to serve it
   to them is the user's call (tailnet, the proxy, a port on this PC).
7. **Clients of both kinds on one display**: two versions of the picture
   at once (HDR and SDR), or one for all?
8. **Settings**: is the SDR conversion fixed, or adjustable (how highlights
   roll off)?

## What happens today

Read from the code; not observed on an HDR display.

- Windows capture asks Desktop Duplication for 8-bit BGRA and nothing else:
  `DuplicateOutput1(device, 0, &[DXGI_FORMAT_B8G8R8A8_UNORM])` in
  `duplicate`, `crates/platform-windows/src/dxgi.rs`. On an HDR desktop
  (16-bit float) Windows converts the image to SDR before we see it. So
  the conversion is not ours: there is nothing in it to tune.
- On systems where only the older `DuplicateOutput` works, an HDR desktop
  is not captured at all: the frame is rejected with "desktop image format
  ... is not BGRA" (same file).
- Everything after capture is 8-bit SDR: the shared screen copy and the
  tile comparison (BGRX), the lossless tiles, the encoder input texture
  (`DXGI_FORMAT_B8G8R8A8_UNORM`), the video stream (marked BT.709, limited
  range, in `crates/encoder/src/ffmpeg.rs`), the client's textures and its
  canvas.
- Linux: X11 and Xvfb have no HDR, so this is a Windows matter for now.

## Browser test (2026-11-11)

Question: can the browser put HDR on a WebGL canvas, and how does a
decoded picture get there? Done by hand with pages of its own; the client
was not touched.

### Method

- This PC's main display (`\\.\DISPLAY1`, 3840x2160) was already in HDR
  mode: 10 bits, SDR white at 280 nits, peak 465 nits (Windows display
  settings and `IDXGIOutput6::GetDesc1`). The second display, the one the
  demo streams, has HDR off.
- Edge 156 (Chromium), a visible window on that display, launched with
  default flags unless said otherwise. No other browser is installed here.
- The pages draw patches of known brightness. A tool outside the browser
  then reads what reached the screen: it duplicates the display asking for
  the 16-bit float image and averages each patch. Values are linear scRGB,
  1.0 = 80 nits; below they are given in nits. Control patches: SDR white
  reads 280 nits, pure red (3.50, 0, 0), 50 % grey 0.76.
- Nobody had to look at the screen: every number below was measured.
- "Fresh" means the browser was restarted for that row; it matters (see
  the worker rows). Worker canvases were measured one per page: with
  several on a page, opaque ones read as the page background in the
  capture.

Scratch files, git-ignored, on this machine only: `.tools/hdr-probe/`.
`src/main.rs` is the tool (`hdr-probe` lists displays with their HDR
state; `hdr-probe capture <display> x,y,w,h ...` reads patches).
`web/` has the pages (`index.html` everything on the page's thread,
`gl.html` WebGL variants, `worker2.html` / `worker3.html` the worker
cases, `precision.html`) and the test pictures. The pictures were made
with FFmpeg (a downloaded build, since removed): one 1280x720 10-bit frame
with five grey bars at 100, 203, 400, 1000 and 4000 nits on the PQ curve,
or a ramp through all 877 luma codes, encoded with libx265 (Main 10) and
SVT-AV1, signalled as BT.2020 PQ or as plain sRGB / BT.709.

### Canvas: where HDR appears

Patches at 1, 1.5 and 3 times SDR white, so 280 / 420 / 840 nits is the
right answer. Fresh browser, default flags:

| canvas | on screen | |
|---|---|---|
| WebGL2, 8-bit (what the client has) | 280 / 280 / 280 | clipped at SDR white |
| WebGL2 with a float drawing buffer, on the page's thread | 280 / 419 / 839 | HDR |
| the same, opaque (`alpha: false`, as the client asks) | 280 / 280 / 280 | the float buffer is refused (`INVALID_OPERATION`) |
| WebGPU, `rgba16float`, tone mapping `extended`, on the page's thread | 280 / 419 / 839 | HDR |
| 2D canvas, `float16` | SDR | its tone mapping stays `standard` |
| CSS colours above white | 280 | clipped |
| WebGL2 float, OffscreenCanvas in a worker, alone | 280 / 280 / 280 | clipped |
| the same with `desynchronized: true` asked | 80 / 95 / 129 | the values go out as raw scRGB (1.0 = 80 nits) |
| WebGPU `extended`, OffscreenCanvas in a worker, alone | 280 / 280 / 280 | clipped |
| worker canvas + a 4 px float WebGL canvas on the page | as alone | no help |
| worker canvas (WebGL float or WebGPU) + a 4 px WebGPU `extended` canvas on the page | 280 / 419 / 839 | HDR |
| 10-bit tiles: an `RGB10_A2` texture with PQ codes, our curve in the shader, worker WebGL float + that WebGPU canvas | 138 / 280 / 554 / 1377 / 5494 | wanted 138 / 280 / 552 / 1379 / 5517 |

- The float drawing buffer is `gl.getExtension('EXT_color_buffer_float')`
  and then `gl.drawingBufferStorage(gl.RGBA16F, width, height)`. No
  tone-mapping call exists for WebGL without flags, and none is needed.
  Values are sRGB-encoded with 1.0 = SDR white and simply go on above 1.
- Values above the display's peak (465 nits) reach the compositor as
  written; the display clips them. The page cannot learn the peak or the
  SDR white level: `screen.highDynamicRangeHeadroom` only exists behind a
  flag. `matchMedia('(dynamic-range: high)')` was true;
  `(video-dynamic-range: high)` was false.
- With `--enable-experimental-web-platform-features` WebGL also gets
  `drawingBufferToneMapping` and the `srgb-linear` and `rec2100-pq`
  colour spaces. A PQ canvas showed 200 / 272 / 386 for 203 / 305 / 609
  nits: absolute brightness, squeezed towards the peak by the browser.
  Nothing we need.

### Decoded video: what the browser does to it

The bars picture, signalled as HDR (BT.2020 PQ), decoded with WebCodecs
(`optimizeForLatency`); 100 / 203 / 400 / 1000 / 4000 nits went in:

| way onto the page | hardware decoder (HEVC) | software decoder (AV1) |
|---|---|---|
| `<video>` fed with the frames (`MediaStreamTrackGenerator`) | 71 / 145 / 244 / 325 / 463 | 83 / 170 / 307 / 465 / 465 |
| 2D canvas `drawImage` | 68 / 139 / 175 / 210 / 280 | 68 / 139 / 207 / 277 / 280 |
| WebGL `texImage2D` (what the client does) | 61 / 83 / 107 / 147 / 221 | 134 / 280 / 280 / 280 / 280 |
| WebGPU `importExternalTexture` | 141 / 162 / 182 / 210 / 252 | 123 / 143 / 154 / 162 / 162 |

- Only `<video>` shows HDR. The browser maps it to the display its own
  way: with the stream's HDR10 metadata (4000-nit mastering) a 203-nit
  white came out at 145 nits, without metadata (the AV1 stream) at 170;
  the page's own white is 280.
- Every other way squeezes the picture into SDR, each with a different
  curve. The first two rows follow the stream's metadata, the last two
  the decoder: hardware AV1 gave the AV1 column in the first two rows and
  the HEVC column in the last two. The WebGL hardware result is the PQ
  codes untouched, shown as if they were sRGB; the software one was
  converted and clipped at SDR white.
- So a stream signalled as HDR is not something to build on: what the page
  receives depends on the API, on the decoder and on the metadata.

The same samples signalled as ordinary SDR (sRGB / BT.709), with the PQ
curve applied by our own shader (relative to a 203-nit white) on an HDR
canvas; 138 / 280 / 552 / 1379 / 5517 is the right answer:

| | on screen |
|---|---|
| WebGL float canvas, hardware HEVC | 133 / 275 / 537 / 1323 / 5369 |
| WebGPU `extended` canvas, hardware HEVC | 133 / 275 / 537 / 1329 / 5368 |
| WebGPU `extended` canvas, software AV1 | 135 / 279 / 543 / 1342 / 5284 |

- This works everywhere it was tried and gives the brightness we ask for,
  2-4 % low: the browser returns the top code as 253 of 255 (254 with the
  software decoder). A calibration, or a transfer that the browser passes
  exactly, has to be found when implementing.
- A hardware frame cannot be copied out as planes
  (`VideoFrame.format` is null); a software AV1 frame can (`I420P10`,
  1 ms for 720p).
- Edge decoded HEVC Main 10 in hardware, and AV1 10-bit in hardware and
  in software.

### Precision

A ramp with all 877 luma codes of a 10-bit picture went in; distinct
levels that came back:

| stream signalled as | WebGL `texImage2D`, any texture format, float included | WebGPU `importExternalTexture` |
|---|---|---|
| sRGB / BT.709, hardware | 254 | 254 |
| sRGB / BT.709, software | 255 | 255 |
| BT.2020 PQ, hardware | 256 | 533 |
| BT.2020 PQ, software | 126 | 149 |
| BT.2020 HLG, hardware | 256 | 533 |
| BT.2020 with an SDR transfer, hardware | 256 | 550 |
| BT.2020 with an SDR transfer, software | 255 | 255 |

- Through WebGL a decoded picture has 8 bits, whatever was sent.
- Through WebGPU it has more than 8 only for hardware frames signalled as
  HDR or wide gamut, which are the ones the browser alters (previous
  section). The wide-gamut row may be usable; whether its values pass
  unchanged was not checked.
- The PQ curve in 8 bits is coarse: between the 100 and 203-nit bars one
  code is a 3.8 % step in brightness (computed), against about 1 % for
  8-bit sRGB near white. Hence open question 3.
- Pixel data we upload ourselves is not affected: the 10-bit tiles above
  arrived exactly.

### What it means

- HDR on a WebGL canvas: yes, in Edge on Windows, with a float drawing
  buffer. The canvas must not be opaque, and since the client draws in a
  worker the page needs a WebGPU HDR canvas next to it (or the drawing
  moves to the page's thread).
- Lossless HDR tiles: yes, exactly, as 10-bit textures.
- HDR video: yes, signalled as SDR with our own curve, at 8 bits; or in a
  `<video>` element at full precision with the browser's brightness.
- Not known: any other browser or device.

## What would have to be done

A work list for whoever picks this up, not an agreed design.

Sunshine (task 003) does both halves and is a reference for the server
side: it asks duplication for 16-bit float first, has one set of shaders
for PQ output and another for linear to sRGB, sends HDR only when the
client asks and the display is in HDR, and needs 10-bit HEVC or AV1 for
it (`src/platform/windows/display_vram.cpp:~2025-2046`,
`src/platform/windows/display_base.cpp:~772-840`,
`src/video_colorspace.cpp:49-105` in `.tools/ref/Sunshine`). It avoids
the 10-bit integer capture format because it "seems to actually just
clamp" the float values.

### Server, both halves

1. **HDR state of the display.** Whether it is on, the SDR white level
   (`DisplayConfigGetDeviceInfo`, `DISPLAYCONFIG_SDR_WHITE_LEVEL`) and the
   peak (`IDXGIOutput6::GetDesc1`); the test tool reads all three. The
   state can change while streaming (the user switches HDR, moves the SDR
   brightness slider): reopen the capture, as for a mode change.
2. **Capture in float.** Ask duplication for
   `DXGI_FORMAT_R16G16B16A16_FLOAT` first, BGRA as today when the display
   is not in HDR. The capture texture and the copies of changed regions
   become 16-bit float (8 bytes per pixel on the GPU).
3. **A conversion pass on the GPU**, right after capture, from linear
   scRGB (1.0 = 80 nits). It replaces what Windows does today and has one
   or two outputs, below.
4. **Tile comparison.** Run it on one of the converted images, not on the
   float one, so that the CPU path keeps reading 4 bytes per pixel. The
   conversion must give the same output for the same input every frame
   (no changing dither), or every tile would count as changed.
5. **Cursor.** Its shape comes from duplication as SDR and is drawn by the
   client: no change expected. To check on an HDR desktop.

### Better SDR (no client change)

6. The conversion pass writes the 8-bit BGRA image the rest of the
   pipeline already uses: divide by the SDR white level, roll highlights
   off above white instead of clipping (the curve and its settings are
   open questions 1 and 8), bring colours into sRGB, encode, dither with a
   fixed pattern.
7. Nothing else changes: tiles, video and client stay as they are. This
   half can be done and judged alone.

### HDR to the client

8. **A second output of the conversion pass**: the picture relative to SDR
   white (1.0 = the host's SDR white, so the desktop's white lands on the
   client's white), in a transfer of our choice, 10 bits.
9. **Lossless tiles in 10 bits** (`R10G10B10A2`, 4 bytes per pixel as
   today): the shared screen copy, LZ4 / zstd and the client upload take
   them as they take BGRX now; they compress somewhat worse. The client
   uploads them as `RGB10_A2` textures.
10. **Video.** Per open question 3: 8 bits with our curve, signalled as
    SDR so that the browser leaves the samples alone; or 10-bit HEVC /
    AV1 signalled as PQ for a `<video>` element. H.264 can only carry the
    first. The encoder input becomes a second texture (the HDR output),
    in 10 bits for the second variant (`P010`; NVENC HEVC Main 10 and AV1
    take it, AMF and QuickSync unverified, x264 no).
11. **Protocol.** HELLO says that the picture is HDR, the transfer in use
    and the host's white and peak levels; the client says whether it can
    show HDR (with its display check, which can change when the window
    moves to another screen). A switch of HDR on the host is a new HELLO,
    like a display change.
12. **SDR and HDR clients on one display** (open question 7): both
    outputs kept side by side (twice the shared copy, compared once), or
    one picture for all and HDR only when every client can show it.

### Client

13. **Detect**: `matchMedia('(dynamic-range: high)')`, the float drawing
    buffer (`EXT_color_buffer_float` and `drawingBufferStorage`), and for
    the `<video>` variant the 10-bit decoder. Anything missing: SDR as
    today.
14. **Canvas**: float drawing buffer, `alpha: true` (opaque is refused),
    output sRGB-encoded with 1.0 = SDR white. In the worker this needs the
    WebGPU canvas on the page (open question 5).
15. **Shader**: both layers decoded from our transfer to linear, then
    encoded for the canvas. The lossless layer becomes a 10-bit texture;
    screen moves (the scratch copy) follow its format.
16. **Video frames**: `texImage2D` as today for the 8-bit variant; a
    `<video>` element fed with the decoder's frames for the other.
17. The stats overlay says whether the picture is HDR and why not.

### Order that keeps each step useful

1. Capture in float and the SDR conversion of our own (items 1-7): better
   SDR for everybody, and the float capture the rest needs.
2. Client HDR canvas and 10-bit lossless tiles (9, 11, 13-15): a still
   desktop in HDR.
3. HDR video (10, 16) once open question 3 is decided.

## Testing

- Needs a display in HDR mode. This PC's main display is in HDR; the one
  the demo streams is not. Switching HDR on a display changes what the
  user sees: ask first (`AGENTS.md`).
- The method of the browser test can check the finished feature without
  anybody looking: stream an HDR desktop to a client window on the HDR
  display and read both displays with the float capture; they should
  agree.
- Other browsers and devices: open `.tools/hdr-probe/web/index.html`
  there (HTTPS or localhost). Without the capture tool the check is by
  eye: in an HDR-capable row the right-hand bands are brighter than the
  white reference at the top left.

## Not verified

- Any browser other than Edge 156 on Windows: Chrome, Firefox, Safari,
  macOS, phones.
- How Windows' own SDR conversion looks next to any alternative: nothing
  was captured or compared.
- Whether the WebGPU canvas that switches a worker's canvas to HDR may be
  invisible or outside the window, and how it behaves over time (tried:
  4 px, visible, a few seconds).
- Why a worker's canvas with `desynchronized` shows raw scRGB, and whether
  today's opaque canvas is unaffected on an HDR display in real use.
- The cost of a float drawing buffer and of `alpha: true` for the client's
  latency (the opaque, desynchronized canvas was chosen for it).
- Latency of the `<video>` path.
- Whether the browser passes a wide-gamut SDR stream's values unchanged
  (the one case with more than 8 bits that is not tone-mapped).
- Banding of 8-bit HDR video in practice: the 3.8 % step is computed, no
  picture was judged.
- That NVENC, AMF and QuickSync through FFmpeg accept 10-bit input from a
  D3D11 texture.
